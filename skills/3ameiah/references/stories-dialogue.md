# Stories, Narration, and Dialogue

Use this playbook for fiction, children's stories, scripts with characters, dialogue, narrative localization, and voice-sensitive rewrites.

## Establish the contract

1. Identify audience, age, genre, setting, narrator, characters, selected mode, and allowed creative freedom.
2. Lock plot beats, chronology, point of view, relationships, names, pronouns, forms of address, and world facts.
3. Build a compact voice map for each narrator and speaker: age, relationship, rhythm, vocabulary, regional evidence, emotional state, and phrases to preserve.
4. Decide whether narration and dialogue use the same register. They often should not.

## Adapt the story

- Preserve events, causal links, characterization, emotional arc, and speaker intent unless creative changes are requested.
- Keep narration clear and usually lighter than dialogue; let characters carry stronger voice when appropriate.
- Give characters distinct rhythms without caricaturing region, class, age, education, or identity.
- Preserve source-supported regional speech; do not invent or normalize it into generic Cairo dialect.
- Simplify concepts for children without baby talk, broken grammar, or removing emotional truth.
- Keep jokes, wordplay, rhymes, and cultural references only when they still serve the same function; adapt by effect rather than literally when allowed.
- Attribute dialogue clearly and maintain consistent tense, point of view, and forms of address.

## Continuity pass

- Check name spelling, pronouns, age, setting, time, objects, abilities, relationships, and chronology.
- Check who knows what at each point and whether dialogue reveals information too early.
- Check emotional transitions and ensure stronger dialect did not change a character's hostility, confidence, or status.
- Compare each plot beat against the source and flag any intentional creative deviation.
- Read dialogue aloud for speakability and distinct voice; read narration for flow and clarity.

## Edge handling

- Do not add backstory merely to justify dialect choices.
- Do not turn `3ameiah-friendly` into fake intimacy, pet names, forced jokes, or a class stereotype.
- If an exact quote, song lyric, poem, or copyrighted passage is in scope, preserve only within applicable user and policy limits; do not silently rewrite a quoted line as if exact.
- If a character is non-Egyptian, preserve source voice and intelligibility rather than forcing Egyptian markers into their dialogue.
- For illustrated books, subtitles, or formatted documents, also load the matching media/document playbook and validate text-image or timing alignment.
